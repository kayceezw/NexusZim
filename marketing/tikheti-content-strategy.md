### Pillar 1: "Finally, Zimbabwe Has This" (Cultural Ownership)

**Theme:** The argument that Tikheti is not a foreign product adapted for Zimbabwe — it is something Zimbabwe built for itself, from the name outward. This pillar claims cultural territory without being kitschy or performative. It answers the unspoken question every urban Zimbabwean has when they first encounter a new local platform: "Is this actually for us, or is this someone's Eventbrite clone?"

**Rationale:** The name "Tikheti" is the platform's most immediate brand signal. It is recognisable, warm, and immediately Zimbabwean. This pillar amplifies that signal into a content programme. The Zimbabwe event scene is vibrant, creative, and increasingly aspirational. Young urban Zimbabweans have watched international platforms dominate their digital lives. Tikheti is the first event-specific platform that is theirs. That is a story worth telling loudly, confidently, and repeatedly.

**Content types:** Brand film clips, social statics and short-form video that centre Zimbabwean cultural moments (Harare nightlife, Bulawayo arts, heritage events, music festivals), founder and team narrative, "made in Zimbabwe" brand storytelling, cultural calendar content.

**Audience primarily served:** Event-goers, specifically urban 18–35 Zimbabweans and diaspora. Also serves organiser acquisition by showing that the platform understands the culture it is built for.

**3 specific content ideas:**

1. **"The Name Tells You Everything" — Brand origin film clip (60 seconds, Instagram/TikTok/Facebook).** Opens on a crowded Harare event venue — lights, noise, bodies moving. Text appears over the crowd: "What do you call it when you finally have your ticket?" Cut to the Tikheti logo on a dark background. Voiceover (warm, confident, male or female — not announcement-voice): "We didn't borrow a name from somewhere else. Tikheti. Because that's what it is. And we built the whole thing to match." End frame: tikheti.co.zw [NEEDS CONFIRMATION — confirm domain] with the tagline "Zimbabwe's own."

2. **Monthly "Zim Event Culture" editorial carousel (Instagram, 6 slides).** Each month, a themed carousel celebrating a specific genre of Zimbabwean event life — one month it's Harare nightlife, next month it's Bulawayo jazz, next it's church events that turn into community anchors. Slide 1 is a full-bleed image (near-black wash, cinematic), Slides 2–5 are editorial copy about the scene with no product push, Slide 6 is the soft Tikheti close: "Events like this deserve a platform that understands them. Tikheti." Photography sourced from Zimbabwean photographers — never stock.

3. **"The Ticket Queue" — 30-second TikTok/Reels.** A single visual sequence: someone standing in a long cash-ticket queue at an event. Long shot. Then cut to: someone on their phone, Tikheti open, ticket downloaded, walking straight through. No voiceover needed. Text overlay: "The queue is optional now." This is an evergreen clip that works pre-launch as a teaser and post-launch as a product ad.

### Pillar 2: "The Organiser's Edge" (Organiser Intelligence & Value)

**Theme:** Event organisers are the supply side that makes Tikheti work. This pillar is entirely focused on their professional reality — the chaos of informal ticket sales, the money that leaks, the knowledge that disappears after every event — and how Tikheti changes the equation. The Organiser Intelligence Panel (notes, post-mortems, vendor contacts) is a genuine product differentiator that no competitor offers in this market. This pillar makes that feature visible and emotionally resonant.

**Rationale:** Organisers are skeptical by nature. They have been pitched platforms before. The way to earn their attention is not with feature lists — it is with recognition. "We understand your job is not just the night of the event. It's everything that happens before, and everything you lose the morning after." This pillar opens with recognition, then offers the tool.

**Content types:** Organiser-facing LinkedIn posts, Facebook long-form, WhatsApp broadcast series, short-form video walkthroughs of the intelligence panel (once in beta), case study format posts (pre-launch: hypothetical/illustrative; post-launch: real data), "Organiser's Playbook" email series.

**Audience primarily served:** Event organisers (all categories — concerts, corporate, community, sports, nightlife, social).

**3 specific content ideas:**

1. **"What You Lose the Morning After" — LinkedIn/Facebook post.** Body copy: "The event was a success. The venue was full. The crowd loved it. And three weeks later, you're starting from scratch. You can't remember which caterer you used for the VIP lounge. The notes from your vendor WhatsApp group are buried under 400 messages. The number for the lighting rig company is in someone's phone who no longer works for you. Every event organiser in Zimbabwe knows this feeling. Tikheti's Organiser Intelligence Panel means your next event starts where your last one ended. Notes, contacts, post-mortem — all attached to the event, permanently. The knowledge stays with the platform, not the person." Close: "We're launching soon. If you run events in Zimbabwe, you should be first in." [CTA: organiser waitlist link]

2. **"Track the Money" — Instagram carousel (5 slides), static graphic series.** Slide 1: "How many tickets did you actually sell last Saturday?" Dark background, white type. Slide 2: "Cash at the door: no record. WhatsApp payments: screenshots in a folder somewhere. Transfer receipts: maybe." Slide 3: "On Tikheti: real-time sales dashboard, ticket-by-ticket. You know exactly where you are at 8pm, midnight, and the morning after." Slide 4: "Your revenue. Finally visible." Slide 5: Tikheti logo + organiser waitlist CTA. Aesthetic: near-black (#0a0a0a) backgrounds, Space Grotesk type, off-white (#f4f4f4) primary text, accent colour pull for data figures.

3. **"The Event That Taught Me Everything" — organiser story format (blog post / Facebook long-form / email).** A fictional-but-realistic first-person narrative from an organiser's perspective: a mid-size Harare concert, cash ticket chaos, a no-show by an untracked vendor, post-event revenue uncertainty. The piece does not name Tikheti until the penultimate paragraph. The final line: "Everything I learned that night is now in my Tikheti notes panel. I will not make those mistakes again." ### Pillar 3: "Your Ticket, Protected" (Trust & Accountability)

**Theme:** The event-goer trust problem in Zimbabwe is real. People have paid for tickets on WhatsApp and the event did not happen. They have arrived at venues to find the event cancelled with no refund mechanism. They have bought from fake accounts. This pillar establishes Tikheti as the platform that carries accountability — not just a sales channel, but a trust layer. It speaks plainly about the problem before offering the solution.

**Rationale:** Trust-building content is not marketing in the traditional sense. It is proof. This pillar works by naming the specific anxieties that real event-goers in Zimbabwe carry, and then explaining — concretely, not aspirationally — what Tikheti does about each one. This is the content that earns credibility and drives first-ticket purchases from cautious buyers.

**Content types:** Trust-narrative social posts, FAQ formats, "how Tikheti works" explainer series, refund and accountability policy explainers (once policy is confirmed), "What happens if..." scenario content, platform security signals.

**Audience primarily served:** Event-goers (first-time buyers, cautious buyers, diaspora buyers purchasing remotely). Also serves organiser acquisition by showing legitimacy — serious organisers want a serious platform.

**3 specific content ideas:**

1. **"We've All Been There" — Social static / WhatsApp card.** Split copy block: "Paid for the ticket. Showed up. Event cancelled. No refund. No explanation. No accountability." Below a thin dividing rule: "On Tikheti, every event has an organiser of record. If something changes, you hear from us first. Not from a WhatsApp status update at 9pm." This is a card — shareable, data-light, works in a forward. Available in English and optionally in a Shona variant for cultural resonance on WhatsApp chains. [Flag for legal review — confirm what accountability claim the platform can make at launch re: refunds/organiser vetting]

2. **"How a Tikheti Ticket Works" — Step-by-step Instagram carousel (7 slides).** Slide by slide: You find an event on Tikheti. You see the organiser's profile. You check the event details. You pay (EcoCash or card). You receive your ticket (QR code). At the event, it's scanned. If anything changes before the event, you are notified by the platform. Final slide: "Seven steps. Zero WhatsApp groups. One platform responsible." Clean, dark aesthetic, JetBrains Mono accents for the numbered steps.

3. **"Ask Us Anything" — Monthly live format (Instagram/Facebook Live or WhatsApp voice broadcast).** Pre-launch: a Q&A session hosted by KC or the Tikheti team responding to submitted questions about how the platform works, what happens if an event is cancelled, how diaspora payments work. This is not a polished production — it is real, direct, and builds genuine platform relationship before the first ticket is sold. Post-launch, this becomes a recurring feature.

### Pillar 4: "The Event Is Everything" (Event Culture & Experience Editorial)

**Theme:** Tikheti is not just a ticketing utility. It is a brand that loves Zimbabwean event culture. This pillar is pure editorial — celebrating events, scenes, performers, and the communities built around live experiences in Zimbabwe. It is the content that makes people follow the account even when they are not actively buying a ticket.

**Rationale:** The brand aesthetic is cinematic and aspirational. The audience is urban, culturally engaged, and exposed to international standards. To earn sustained attention (not just transactional clicks), Tikheti needs to be a voice worth listening to on the subject of Zimbabwean events. This pillar builds that voice. It is also the primary driver of organic social reach — cultural celebration content shares and engages far more than product content.

**Content types:** Event preview and review content, artist/performer spotlights, "this weekend in Harare / Bulawayo" editorial series, behind-the-scenes event content, cultural calendar features, event photography showcase.

**Audience primarily served:** Event-goers, primarily 18–35 urban Zimbabweans and diaspora. Secondary: builds platform brand awareness among organisers who want their events featured.

**3 specific content ideas:**

1. **"This Weekend" — Weekly editorial card (Instagram Stories + Feed, Friday posting).** A curated visual list of 3–5 upcoming events in Harare and Bulawayo, formatted as an editorial card series. Each card: event name, date, venue, short editorial line (not a description copy-paste — an actual editorial take: "The Bulawayo jazz scene does not wait for permission. This one is the real thing."). Pre-launch: uses publicly announced events as editorial content, no ticketing yet. Post-launch: links directly to Tikheti listings. This becomes the city guide people save every Friday.

2. **"After the Last Song" — Long-form post-event editorial (Instagram caption / Facebook post / blog).** After a major Zimbabwe event, Tikheti publishes a short editorial piece — 150 to 200 words — about the event's cultural significance. Not a review. Not a recap. A reflection. Example (hypothetical): After a major Harare music event: "There's a specific kind of quiet that follows a great show. The kind that feels loud. Last night at [venue], [artist] did not give a performance — they gave the room something to carry home. This is what events are supposed to feel like. This is what we're building Tikheti for." Always ends with the Tikheti name, never a hard CTA. Let the brand speak through what it values.

3. **"Scene" — Monthly long-form feature (email newsletter / Facebook / LinkedIn).** A deep-dive into one corner of Zimbabwe's event culture each month: the Harare comedy scene, the church event economy, corporate galas as a business, the underground nightlife scene in Bulawayo. Genuinely reported, genuinely editorial. Commissioned from Zimbabwean writers — 400 to 600 words. Tikheti's brand appears in the header and footer but not in the body. The value is in the quality of the writing, which reflects on the platform's credibility and cultural investment.

### Pillar 5: "Diaspora Row" (International Zimbabwean Audience)

**Theme:** Zimbabweans abroad are a distinct and commercially meaningful audience segment for Tikheti. They want to be connected to what is happening at home. They attend events when they visit. They buy tickets as gifts. They send their kids or siblings to events. They follow the Harare and Bulawayo scenes on social media. This pillar speaks to them directly — as people who have never stopped caring about Zimbabwe's events, not as a demographic target.

**Rationale:** Diaspora Zimbabweans are active on Facebook (older cohort, UK/SA/US), Instagram and TikTok (younger cohort), and WhatsApp (all ages). They are also high-converting — if they trust the platform, they will purchase. International payment capability (card payments in GBP, USD, ZAR) is a genuine differentiator that this pillar exists to activate.

**Content types:** Diaspora-specific social posts (Facebook-weighted, also Instagram), "buy a ticket from anywhere" product explainers, diaspora gift-ticket concept content, cultural homesickness-adjacent content that resonates emotionally, WhatsApp broadcast series targeting diaspora WhatsApp groups.

**Audience primarily served:** Zimbabwean diaspora (UK, South Africa, United States, Australia, Canada primary markets).

### Pillar 6: "Platform as Infrastructure" (B2B / Institutional Trust)

**Theme:** This pillar is quieter and more deliberate than the others. It speaks to corporate event buyers, institutional organisers (universities, NGOs, large churches, government-adjacent events), and professional event planning businesses. The message: Tikheti is not a startup experiment. It is infrastructure. It is the layer that should sit under every ticketed event in Zimbabwe.

**Rationale:** Corporate and institutional buyers have budget, volume, and long event calendars. A single corporate client running 6 events a year is more commercially significant than six individual consumers. This pillar positions Tikheti as the professional choice — not just the convenient one. LinkedIn is the primary channel; Facebook and email support it.

**Content types:** LinkedIn thought leadership, case studies (post-launch), data/insight posts about the event industry, organiser intelligence panel deep-dives, corporate package positioning (once confirmed), feature announcement posts.

**Audience primarily served:** Corporate event planners, institutional organisers, professional event companies, sports bodies, conference organisers.

**3 specific content ideas:**

1. **"Event Data Is Business Intelligence" — LinkedIn article.** An 800-word argument that the data generated by events — who came, how they bought, what sold out, what did not — is as valuable as the event revenue itself. Current informal methods destroy this data. Tikheti captures it per-event and makes it available to the organiser through the intelligence panel. The article does not name competitors. It names the problem: "The Zimbabwe events industry is running on instinct when it should be running on data." CTA: organiser waitlist + "Let's talk if you run more than three events a year."

2. **"The Organiser Intelligence Panel" — Product feature reveal post (LinkedIn + email).** A structured feature walkthrough, written for a professional audience: what the panel captures, how it works, what decisions it makes easier for the next event. This is not a marketing post — it is a product communication written for people who will evaluate it as a professional tool. Tone is precise and specific, not aspirational. Includes a single illustrative scenario: a corporate conference organiser using post-event notes to pre-negotiate vendor contracts for the following year's event.

3. **"Why Forward-Thinking Event Companies Are Moving Digital" — LinkedIn editorial post.** A market observation piece (not Tikheti-promotional in tone) about the direction of the Zimbabwe events industry: rising event frequency, diaspora engagement with home events, growing urbanite expectations. The conclusion: the infrastructure question is not "if" but "when." Tikheti is named only in the final sentence. This is the kind of piece that earns shares from people who are not yet Tikheti customers.

### Instagram & TikTok

**Audience on channel:** Urban Zimbabweans 18–35, diaspora same age range (Instagram), younger event culture audience on TikTok.

**Cadence:** 4–5 posts per week on Instagram (mix of Feed and Stories), 2–3 short-form videos per week on TikTok during pre-launch, scaling to daily TikTok post at launch week.

**Content mix:**
- 30% Cultural/editorial (event culture content, "This Weekend" series, scene features)
- 25% Product/brand storytelling (what Tikheti is and does, platform aesthetic reveals)
- 20% Trust content (how it works, what makes a Tikheti ticket different)
- 15% Organiser-facing (the case for listing on Tikheti)
- 10% Diaspora angle

**Format priorities:** Reels and TikTok video are the reach engine. Carousels are the engagement and save engine (saves signal algorithm weight). Static posts are used sparingly — only when the graphic does the work on its own. Stories are daily and conversational — polls, countdowns, questions.

**Visual direction:** Every post enforces the brand aesthetic. Near-black (#0a0a0a) or very dark atmospheric photography as background. Space Grotesk for all digital text overlays. Instrument Serif for editorial display quotes. JetBrains Mono for any data callout or technical element. Off-white (#f4f4f4) primary text. No drop shadows. No clip-art. No stock photography of generic "event" scenes — only real Zimbabwean event photography or brand-original dark-aesthetic graphics.

## PART SEVEN — TONE & VOICE GUIDE (MINI)

---

### 5 "We Say / We Don't Say" Examples

---

**1.**
We say: "Your events. Finally sorted."
We don't say: "Revolutionising Zimbabwe's event ticketing ecosystem."

*Why:* "Finally sorted" is human. "Revolutionising ecosystem" is the language of pitch decks, not of a brand that genuinely understands its audience. Tikheti speaks like the cool older sibling who has already figured out the thing you are struggling with — not like a startup announcing a disruption.

---

**2.**
We say: "You've paid for a ticket that didn't go anywhere before. We know. That's why we built this."
We don't say: "Our platform offers unprecedented trust and accountability for Zimbabwe's event industry."

*Why:* Unprecedentedness is a claim. Recognition is a relationship. The audience doesn't need to be told Tikheti is unprecedented — they need to feel that Tikheti understands their specific experience. Lead with the human experience, not the brand positioning.

---

**3.**
We say: "EcoCash, Visa, Mastercard. You choose."
We don't say: "We offer a suite of flexible, mobile-first payment integrations including EcoCash and major international card networks."

*Why:* Tikheti's audience is smart and mobile-native. They do not need a feature described to them — they need to know it exists and works. Say the thing clearly. Remove every word that is doing no work.

---

**4.**
We say: "Tikheti. Because that's what it is." (or on occasion: "Hona tikheti dzako." — Here are your tickets.)
We don't say: "We embrace our African heritage by choosing a name rooted in our local language."

*Why:* The name is Zimbabwean. Use it. Let it do its own work. Do not explain the cultural decision in copy — that is self-conscious and awkward. The brand uses local language when it fits naturally; it never announces that it's using local language.

---

**5.**
We say: "The event is happening. Your ticket is real. We'll let you know if anything changes."
We don't say: "Tikheti provides multi-channel event status notifications to ensure attendee confidence and reduce uncertainty."

*Why:* The plain version is more powerful. "We'll let you know if anything changes" is a promise. The jargon version is a feature description. Promises build trust. Feature descriptions build brochures.

---

### Tikheti's Relationship to Shona and Ndebele in Copy

**The guiding principle:** Local language in Tikheti copy earns its place when it is genuinely natural — the way it would appear in conversation between Zimbabweans — not when it is deployed to signal cultural credentials.

**Where local language adds authenticity:**
- The brand name itself is the primary local language signal. It does not need to be supplemented with constant Shona/Ndebele phrases to feel Zimbabwean.
- Short, organic phrases in campaign lines: "Hona tikheti dzako" (Here are your tickets) works as a closer on a social card because it is brief, warm, and naturally said. "Mauya" (Welcome / You've arrived) as a WhatsApp community welcome message. These land because they are what a person would actually say.
- WhatsApp content, especially organiser broadcast messages, can carry occasional Shona phrasing in the close or greeting when the list is known to be Shona-speaking: "Tirikufunga nezvemi events yenyu" (We're thinking about your events). This works because WhatsApp is a personal medium.
- Instagram hashtags: #Zimbabwe, #Harare, #Bulawayo always. Occasional Shona/Ndebele cultural hashtags when the content directly references cultural moments — not as standard practice.

**Where local language reads as forced:**
- Multi-line Shona copy in product-explanatory content. Event organisers evaluating a platform professionally need clarity, not cultural performance.
- Inserting Shona phrases into English-primary copy to "prove" cultural alignment. Example of what not to do: "Tikheti is Zimbabwe's own platform — nekuti (because) we understand how events work here." The parenthetical translation signals that the inclusion is performative.
- Ndebele phrases used in Harare-focused content (and vice versa) — Bulawayo audiences notice and it reads as generic "African language" rather than specific cultural awareness. When Ndebele is used, it should be in Bulawayo-specific content.
- Any phrase that a Zimbabwean would only say in their first language, not in natural code-switching with English. Tikheti's audience code-switches fluidly — the brand should reflect that rhythm, not perform it.

**The test:** Read the phrase aloud. Would a young Harare professional say it that way in a conversation? If yes, use it. If it sounds like it was translated, cut it.
